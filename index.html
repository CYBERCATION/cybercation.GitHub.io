A cyber PSA from someone who cares

I work in cyber security, and I see the same scams catch good, sensible people every week. This is a short guide to what's doing the rounds in the UK right now and what to do about each one. You don't need to be technical, you just need to know the patterns.

Scams to watch for

"This is your bank's fraud team"

Someone calls saying there's suspicious activity on your account. They often know your name, your bank, even the last few digits of your card, and the number on your screen may look like the bank's real one. They'll ask you to move money to a "safe account", read out a code, or hand your card to a courier.

What to do: Hang up. Your bank will never ask you to move money to keep it safe, and will never send someone to collect your card. Call 159 from a different phone if you can, or wait a few minutes so the line clears, and it connects you to your real bank.

Parcel, HMRC and "unpaid fine" texts

"Your parcel couldn't be delivered, pay £1.45 to rebook." "You're due a tax refund." "You have an unpaid parking charge." The amounts are small on purpose, because the real goal is your card details.

What to do: Don't tap the link. Forward the text to 7726 (it's free), then delete it. If you're worried it's real, go to the official website or app yourself.

"Hi Mum, I've lost my phone"

A WhatsApp from an unknown number: "This is my new number, my phone broke." A little chat, then an urgent request to pay a bill because their banking app "isn't working yet".

What to do: Call the old number you already have, or ring someone who'd know. Never pay anything until you've actually spoken to them.

AI voice and video clones

A few seconds of someone's voice from social media is now enough to fake a convincing phone call. It sounds like your child, your partner or your parent, in trouble and needing money right now.

What to do: Ask for the family code word. No code word, no money, however real it sounds.

"Just read me the code we've sent"

Those six-digit codes you get by text or in an app are the last lock on your account. Scammers will pose as your bank, your phone company, Amazon or WhatsApp support to get you to read one out.

What to do: Never share a one-time code with anyone, on any channel, for any reason. Nobody legitimate will ever ask for it.

The friendly wrong-number text

"Hi, is this David? Are we still on for dinner?" It feels rude not to reply. That reply starts a slow, friendly chat that can run for weeks before it turns into a romance or an "amazing investment".

What to do: Don't reply, not even to say "wrong number". Block and delete.

Investment and crypto "opportunities"

Fake adverts with celebrities, slick trading apps that show your money growing, a new online friend who's done really well. The early "profits" are fake, and you only find out when you try to withdraw.

What to do: Check any firm on the FCA register (register.fca.org.uk) and its warning list before you invest a penny. Guaranteed high returns are the tell.

Too-good-to-be-true online shops

Social media ads for designer goods at 80% off, or a "closing down sale". What arrives is a cheap knock-off, or nothing at all.

What to do: Pay by credit card where you can. For anything between £100 and £30,000, Section 75 makes the card company jointly responsible if it goes wrong. Debit cards and PayPal have weaker chargeback protection, and bank transfers to strangers have the least.

Tech support calls and pop-ups

A call "from Microsoft" or "your broadband provider", or a scary pop-up saying your computer is infected. They want you to install software that gives them remote control.

What to do: Hang up or close the browser. Real companies don't call you about viruses. Never let anyone you don't know remote into your device.

The boss who urgently needs gift cards

An email or message that looks like it's from your manager: they're in a meeting, it's urgent, can you buy some gift cards or pay this invoice and send the codes.

What to do: Pick up the phone and ask them directly, using a number you already have. Then report it to your IT or security team so they can warn others.

Reused passwords

Websites get breached all the time, and the stolen email-and-password lists are traded online. Criminals then try those same details on your email, bank and shopping accounts, because they know most people reuse them.

What to do: Check your email addresses at haveibeenpwned.com. Anywhere you've reused a breached password, change it.

Simple habits that protect you

  • Hang up, look it up

    Never act on a message or call that came to you. However real it looks, stop, then contact the organisation yourself using the app, the website you normally use, or the number on the back of your card. This one habit beats almost every scam on this page.

  • Have a family code word

    Agree a word with your close family, in person, and never write it in a message. Make it something that sounds natural in conversation. If someone calls sounding like one of you and asking for money, they need to say it.

  • Use a password manager

    Let it create and remember a different strong password for every site, so you only need to remember one. Your phone already has one built in (Apple Passwords or Google Password Manager), or use something like 1Password or Bitwarden.

  • Turn on two-step verification

    Especially on your email, bank and social media. An authenticator app or a passkey is stronger than a text message code, but any of them is far better than nothing.

  • Slow down when it's urgent

    Pressure is the scammer's main tool: "act now", "don't tell anyone", "your account will be closed". A genuine organisation will always let you take ten minutes to check.

  • Keep an eye on your credit file

    Check your free credit reports from Experian, Equifax and TransUnion now and then, through apps like ClearScore or Credit Karma. If your identity has already been misused, Cifas Protective Registration adds a warning flag to your name.

If it happens to you

Act fast and don't be embarrassed. These scams are run by professionals and they fool clever people every day.
